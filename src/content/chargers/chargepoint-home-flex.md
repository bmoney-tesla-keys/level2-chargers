## What it is, and who it is for

ChargePoint Home Flex is ChargePoint’s wall-mounted Level 2 home charger (model family CPH50). The company sells it as a station that can charge “any EV on the market, including your Tesla,” in either an SAE J1772 or SAE J3400 (NACS) version. The same ChargePoint app used for the company’s public stations also schedules and monitors the home unit.

It is a fit for a homeowner who wants a smart charger, is willing to pay toward the higher end of the category (CNET’s one-word con is “Pricey”; Car and Driver lists “one of the most expensive” as a con), and either already uses ChargePoint in public or wants utility-rate tracking in one app. It is less of a fit if you want the cheapest capable Level 2 unit, a tighter-than-NEMA-3R outdoor rating, or load sharing between two home chargers — none of the sources below document residential power-sharing on Home Flex.

This page does not assign a score. The verdict above is a synthesis of the cited manufacturer documents and the two independent roundups we were able to read in full.

## Key specs (verified)

Figures below come from ChargePoint’s April 2025 CPH50 datasheet unless noted.

- **Maximum output:** 12 kW (240 V AC × 50 A). Output is adjustable in the mobile app to 16 A, 24 A, 32 A, 40 A, 48 A, or 50 A.
- **Plug-in limit:** NEMA plug-in models are limited to 32 A or 40 A output. ChargePoint’s product page states the NEMA 6-50 and NEMA 14-50 versions “deliver up to 40 A of power.”
- **Input / install:** NEMA 6-50, NEMA 14-50, or hardwire. Hardwired units work with circuits rated 20 A to 80 A and can deliver up to 50 A. Plug-in versions work with 40 A or 50 A circuits.
- **Required breaker (manufacturer rule of thumb):** a dedicated dual-pole breaker rated for 125% of the maximum load (the datasheet’s example is a 50 A breaker for 40 A output).
- **Wiring:** three-wire — L1, L2, plus earth; no neutral.
- **Charging cable:** 23 ft (7,010.4 mm).
- **Connector:** SAE J1772 or SAE J3400 (NACS), sold as separate configurations. ChargePoint also lists a self-install NACS conversion kit (23 ft, 50 A cable plus holster insert). NACS cables include a handle button that opens a NACS charge-port door.
- **Network:** 2.4 / 5 GHz Wi-Fi (802.11 a/b/g/n). Order codes also list Bluetooth. Firmware updates over the air. Local storage for up to 90 days of session data (100 sessions) if the network drops.
- **Enclosure:** Type 3R per UL 50E, indoor or outdoor.
- **Safety listings:** UL and cUL; UL 2594, UL 2231-1, UL 2231-2; NEC Article 625. ENERGY STAR certified.
- **Operating temperature:** −30 °C to 50 °C (−22 °F to 122 °F).
- **Size and weight:** 11.19 in high × 7.06 in wide × 5.20 in deep; 13.8 lb (6.3 kg). ChargePoint’s Before You Buy page gives the same outline (7 in wide, 11 in high, 5.2 in deep, and 7 in deep with the connector docked).
- **Warranty:** 3-year parts warranty on current CPH50 order codes; the product page repeats a 3-year warranty. CNET also lists a 3-year warranty.

ChargePoint’s installation FAQ publishes this amperage table. Range-per-hour numbers are the manufacturer’s estimates and will vary by vehicle.

| 24 A | 18 mi / 29 km | 5.8 kW | 30 A | No | Yes |

One discrepancy to flag: CNET described peak output as “up to 50 A (on a 60 A circuit).” ChargePoint’s table assigns a 60 A breaker to the 48 A setting and a 70 A / 80 A breaker to 50 A. We treat ChargePoint’s table as the electrical reference.

## Smart features

ChargePoint’s product and support pages say Home Flex is Wi-Fi enabled and managed in the ChargePoint app (a ChargePoint account is required). Documented app capabilities include scheduling charging around local utility rates, starting charging remotely, seeing miles added for a chosen car model, and tracking home and public charging in one place. ChargePoint says the station will still follow a set schedule if Wi-Fi later drops.

Car and Driver, after months of use on several test EVs, called the app user-friendly, praised its charge-rate graphs, and wrote that it shows total charge time and energy for each session. They singled out “the most nuanced cost tracking,” including the ability to pick from dozens of local utility rate plans and estimate cost “down to the penny,” even on a variable rate. They also liked the built-in cord notch and the centered connector dock, which avoids a separate cable hook.

CNET notes that the home station can be monitored and controlled over Wi-Fi, and that the same app helps find and access ChargePoint’s public network away from home. CNET also highlights a replaceable connector and cable, so a J1772 unit can later take a NACS cable (or the reverse) without replacing the box.

## What independent reviews said

**Car and Driver** (hands-on test; page updated November 2, 2026) listed these specs in its Home Flex card: 16 / 24 / 32 / 40 / 48 / 50 A (3.8 / 5.8 / 7.7 / 9.6 / 11.5 / 12.0 kW), a 23-foot cord, built-in cord management, and a NEMA 3R outdoor rating. Pros were remote monitoring and control, nuanced cost tracking, and the shared public-charging app. Cons were that it is one of the most expensive units they tested, that its outdoor rating is lesser than some others, and that lower output settings require a hardwired installation — which matches ChargePoint’s own plug-in vs. hardwire table. Elsewhere in the same article, Car and Driver notes that a NEMA 3R rating is intended for outdoor use but “does not mean watertight.”

**CNET** named Home Flex “Best EV charger overall runner-up” in its home EV charger guide (page titled for 2026; byline update May 11, 2024). Pros: “Extremely compact design; replaceable connector/cable; up to 50A (on a 60A circuit).” Con: “Pricey.” CNET’s spec line: SAE J1772 or NACS, 23 ft cable, up to 12 kW at 50 A / 240 V, 3-year warranty, hardwired or plug-in, and a phone app.

## Honest trade-offs

**Documented strengths**

- App-selectable 16–50 A range, with hardwire or two common 240 V plug options, so the same family of hardware can match different panels.
- J1772 and NACS SKUs, plus a documented NACS conversion kit and a replaceable cable (CNET).
- ChargePoint app shared with the public network; Car and Driver’s strongest praise is the cost-tracking and session display.
- ENERGY STAR, UL listings, a 3-year parts warranty, and a 23-foot cable that ChargePoint says stays pliable in cold weather.

**Documented drawbacks**

- Price: both CNET and Car and Driver call out cost relative to the rest of their tests.
- Outdoor rating is NEMA 3R, not a tighter NEMA 4 / 4X rating some competitors advertise; Car and Driver lists this as a con.
- Plug-in installs cannot use the 16 A, 24 A, 48 A, or 50 A settings — those require hardwire (ChargePoint FAQ and Car and Driver).
- CNET’s “50 A on a 60 A circuit” wording does not match ChargePoint’s 70 A / 80 A breaker note for the 50 A setting. Have an electrician follow the datasheet and local code, not a review blurb.

Installation cost, utility rebates, and whether a panel upgrade is needed are home-specific. None of the sources above replace a licensed electrician.
